# HCDE 411: Information Visualization

**University of Washington · Department of Human Centered Design & Engineering**
Spring 2026 · 5 credits · In person
Instructor: Brock Craft

HCDE 411 covers the design principles, perceptual foundations, and techniques used to visualize, explore, and analyze large data sets. Students work with real data and two tools, **Tableau** and the Python library **Altair**, and spend the quarter designing, building, testing, and documenting an interactive visualization in a team of four.

There is no textbook and there are no exams. Students learn through short readings, in-class activities, take-home labs, individual assignments, and a ten-week group project.

---

## Learning objectives

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

- Articulate the basic data types and effective visual mappings
- Describe the key design guidelines and techniques for the visual display of information, including their relationship to human perception
- Apply fundamental data visualization techniques and theoretical principles to real-world problems
- Critically analyze the design and presentation of digital information
- Design static and interactive visualizations that present information or enable data exploration, using real data and a human-centered process
- Incorporate visualization techniques into human-computer interfaces

## Grading

| Category | Weight |
|---|---|
| In-class exercises and participation | 35% |
| Group project (Visualization Concept) | 30% |
| Individual assignments | 20% |
| Take-home labs | 15% |

Labs are graded for completion. Late work loses 10% per day; extensions are available if requested before the due date. Teams may revise and resubmit a project milestone (except the final deliverables) within one week of receiving a grade.

**AI use:** AI tools are allowed for brainstorming, polishing, and debugging Tableau or Altair. Rubrics are applied as though the student did the work, so analytical writing should be the student's own.

## Individual assignments

| Assignment | Week | What students do |
|---|---|---|
| **A1: Good and Bad Visualizations** | 2 | Find one effective and one ineffective static visualization from a magazine, newspaper, journal, or poster aimed at a general audience. Explain each using course vocabulary: variable types, visual mappings, expressiveness, and effectiveness. |
| **A2: Data Profile** | 3 | Locate a data set (ideally 5,000+ rows) on a topic of interest and write a profile for a teammate who has never seen it: source, structure, scale, variable types, and the relationships visualization could reveal. Often a candidate data set for the project. |
| **A3: Static Visualization Critique** | 4 | Critique one complex static visualization in 600–800 words using the class evaluation checklist and cited principles from readings and lectures, then sketch a proposed revision. |
| **Reading reflections** | 2–4 | Annotate readings on marks and channels and on tidy data in Hypothesis and bring questions to small-group discussion. |
| **Tableau Projects** | 6 | Watch an end-to-end Tableau build, answer questions on its techniques (calculated fields, containers, dashboard layout), and rebuild one of its charts with specified changes. |

## Take-home labs

Short, completion-graded exercises that prepare students for the next class session.

- **Data Cleaning Lab** (Week 2): Download World Bank World Development Indicators data and clean it to a specified tidy format: remove administrative rows, rename series, recode missing values, and fix year headers.
- **Altair Labs 1–5** (Weeks 3–5): Jupyter notebooks covering an introduction to Altair, visual encoding channels, data transformation, interactive visualization, and publishing.
- **Tableau Labs 1–5** (Weeks 5–8): Data preparation, basic charts, interactivity, dashboards, and storyboards/publishing.
- **Make Your Own Deceptive Visualization** (Week 9): Teams take one of their project visualizations and make a subtly misleading static version using specific techniques from lecture. The class then votes on which version of each pair is honest and discusses how the deception works.

## Group project: Visualization Concept

Teams of four design an interactive visualization on a topic they care about, drawing on data they find or construct. A well-scoped project can be completed by four people working about 50 hours each over six weeks. Milestones follow a human-centered design process:

| Milestone | Week | Deliverable |
|---|---|---|
| **P1: Project Proposal** | 4 | Concept and goals, intended users and tasks, data sources, and sketches (under 5 pages). |
| **P1a: Exploratory Visual Analysis** | 6 | In pairs, pose at least three questions (comparative, correlation, geographic, or temporal), explore the project data in a visualization tool, and report findings. Serves as a stress test of the data set. |
| **P2: Revised Proposal** | 6 | Proposal revised in response to feedback, with an account of what changed and why. |
| **P3a/P3b: Collaborative Critique** | 7–8 | Teams pair up in a client–consultant exercise. The client team briefs its progress and challenges; the consultant team writes a report with at least three recommendations, each stating the problem, the proposed solution, and the rationale. Roles then reverse. Draws on *The Trusted Advisor*. |
| **P4: Design Evaluation** | 9 | A usability test plan with three tasks, lo-fi prototype sessions with three outside participants, and a synthesis of results and planned changes. |
| **Final Presentation: The Bazaar** | 10 | Marketplace-style demo at a table for circulating peers and reviewers, covering goals, data, design process, usability findings, and future improvements. |
| **P5: Final Visualization** | 10 | A working demo with source code, including at least three interactive visualizations (beyond tooltips), two static visualizations, at least one built in Altair, professional labeling, and explanatory text. |
| **P6: Final Documentation** | Finals | A process write-up with artifacts from each stage: assessment of the design against goals, users, and tasks; evaluation of overview/detail views and encodings; and analysis of interactions against Shneiderman's information-seeking tasks or Munzner's actions. |

## In-class activities

Most class sessions include a graded collaborative activity, such as data manifestations and encodings, data cleaning in spreadsheets, Jupyter and Altair walkthroughs, color palettes, visualization critique, defining tasks and interactions, task abstraction, Five Design Sheets (Roberts et al.), and physical data-sorting exercises.
